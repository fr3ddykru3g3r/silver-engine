# Submission reference workbook — student-authored completion required

This is a structured worksheet, not an initial abstract, paper, poster or video script. Do not copy the audit's interpretations into a submission as student work. The [Society AI guidance](https://sspcdn.blob.core.windows.net/files/Documents/SEP/ISEF/2026/Rules/Generative-AI-Use-Table.pdf) says initial submission documents must be the student's independent work. Rewriting an AI-generated initial draft does not meet that stated requirement. AI-assisted code and graphics require disclosure and a prompt log. The student must interpret the data themselves.

## Live requirements checked on 15 September 2026

[IRIS guidelines](https://iris.exstemplar.com/guidelines.html) list: abstract 250 words; introduction/objective 100–150; innovation 50–100; methodology 150–250; results/conclusions 100–150; acknowledgements/references 50–100; video at most 90 seconds. They also request a full paper and research data book, and a guide. Keep school/city/state identity out of anonymous scientific materials. The live site has mixed cycle/date wording; use the October 3 registration deadline as the conservative planning date and verify the portal. The archival-data age interpretation needs SRC clarification.

[Current ISEF rules](https://www.societyforscience.org/isef/international-rules/rules-for-all-projects/) require student eligibility, approved documentation and honest disclosure. Selection by an affiliated fair is required. Current-year research duration rules govern the work period; do not assume the observation date of every archival measurement is the same as research date. Confirm application with SRC.

## 1. Title worksheet

Write three titles yourself. Each should identify the physical problem, the comparison and the scope. Avoid “revolutionary,” “SOTA,” “validated operational,” “first ever,” and “new theorem.” Check that a reader can tell this is forecast verification research, not a claim to predict every storm. Choose based on accuracy and clarity, not how large the result sounds.

## 2. Abstract worksheet — 250-word limit

Allocate roughly 35 words to the problem, 45 to the question/design, 60 to data and fixed-forecast comparison, 65 to results, and 45 to interpretation/limits. These are planning budgets, not mandatory wording.

Answer in your own words:

1. Which decision is occurrence forecasting answering, and which decision is onset forecasting answering?
2. What exactly was held fixed? What changed?
3. Which one clearly named cohort supplies the displayed numbers?
4. What are the joint model's three TSS values and paired contrasts?
5. What does the identity explain? What does it not prove?
6. Which limitation prevents an operational or independent-validation claim?

Fact check: strict diagnostic values 0.726442, 0.621115, 0.437222; 85 admitted episodes. Original frozen values differ slightly and must carry their original cohort label. Do not mix original intervals with the new diagnostic point estimates. The student's interpretation must be independently reasoned.

## 3. Introduction/objective — 100–150 words

Student explains SEP hazard, the >10 MeV integral proton channel, the 10 pfu operational threshold, and why a daily positive window is not a new physical event. Define the primary question before discussing models. Distinguish motivation from demonstrated benefit. Personally read the scientific sources before writing their treatment.

Self-check: Is this a testable question? Does it avoid implying that all operational forecasting ignores persistence? Is the endpoint a score contrast, rather than an unbounded promise to improve space safety?

## 4. Innovation — 50–100 words

Student separates established mathematics from the specific empirical contribution. Explain the fixed-prediction comparison and traceable mechanism. Do not claim invention of inverse-cluster weighting, event verification or the covariance identity. State what a prior identical study would change about the contribution.

## 5. Methodology — 150–250 words

Use the evidence report to write: source/revision; catalog semantics; feature causality limitations; fit/threshold/score periods; fixed probabilities/thresholds; positive and negative weighting; onset eligibility; paired resampling unit; strict protection boundary. Include enough for the estimand to be reconstructed. Put implementation details and hashes in the paper supplement if the synopsis cannot fit them.

Checklist: negatives unchanged? episode average is not any-alert detection? threshold role separated from score role? historical exposure disclosed? uncertainty conditional on fixed forecasts? no protected outcomes inspected?

## 6. Results/conclusions — 100–150 words

The student selects the minimum numerical evidence needed and interprets it themselves. Use the results tables, not a memorized conclusion. Address sign, magnitude and interval. Explain the no-proton contrast without claiming independence. Do not hide the controller/Phase-II failures; they can be a limitation or supplemental table rather than the headline.

Questions: Is the result an arithmetic identity, an empirical association or a claim about future performance? What could falsify generalization? What does FAR imply for an operator? Which claim are you deliberately declining because evidence is insufficient?

## 7. Acknowledgements/references — 50–100 words

Student creates the bibliography after reading original sources and verifying authors, dates, titles, versions and identifiers. This workbook does not supply a submission-ready bibliography. Credit datasets, software, guides, mentors, and AI assistance accurately. Do not attribute AI-created code/analysis to the student. Keep required anonymity in scientific materials; use official forms for identifying information.

## 8. Full-paper structure

| Section | Student supplies | Evidence to consult |
|---|---|---|
| Anonymous title page | Accurate title and permitted identifiers | Portal requirements |
| Contents | Page/section map | Final manuscript |
| Introduction | Decision, scientific background, question | Personally read primary literature |
| Related work | Fair comparison and novelty limits | Source assessment; own reading notes |
| Data | Provenance, physical semantics, denominators, exclusions | Repository truth and manifests |
| Methods | Estimands, splits, threshold rule, covariance derivation, bootstrap | Mathematical mechanism and code |
| Results | One primary table/figure, uncertainty, controls | Original vs strict diagnostic separated |
| Robustness | Predeclared versus post-hoc checks | Followups JSON and intervals |
| Discussion | Student's interpretation and alternatives | Negative ledger and judge questions |
| Limitations | Exposure, event definitions, causal availability, sample support | Protection/gates |
| Conclusion | Student-authored answer to the question | No additional unsupported claims |
| Acknowledgements/references | Accurate contributions and sources | Student log |
| Supplement | Hashes, software versions, formulas, exclusions, all comparators | Reproduction package |

No new methods or outcomes should appear first in the conclusion. Distinguish new current-year analysis from previous work if this is a continuation.

## 9. Poster layout worksheet

Use three columns: question/physical example; fixed-forecast design and main result; mechanism/uncertainty/limitations. The student writes all explanatory text. Suggested allocation: 20% motivation/design, 45% results and figure, 20% mechanism, 15% limits/contribution. Make the cohort and historical-status labels legible. Show the control and false-alert burden, not only the best TSS.

Figures supplied here are AI-assisted technical graphics from deterministic code and verified aggregate data. Follow the official disclosure requirement. Each figure must retain its cohort footer. A figure without uncertainty is a point-estimate illustration, not an interval claim. Do not label equal-event sensitivity as any-event detection.

## 10. 90-second video timing worksheet

0–15 sec: student explains the physical decision and question.
15–35 sec: student draws one long episode producing multiple daily positives.
35–60 sec: student explains the fixed forecasts and main comparison using one figure.
60–75 sec: student derives the meaning of the covariance, without claiming mathematical novelty.
75–90 sec: student states the strongest limitation and what evidence would challenge their conclusion.

These are prompts, not a script. Record twice, time it, and ask a listener to restate the question. Ensure anonymity and readable labels. Never imply the student built or proved something they only received from this audit.

## 11. Research data book

For each real work session record date/time; question; prior expectation; source/version; actual commands; output hashes; interpretation written before discussion with AI/mentor; failed attempts; assistance received; next decision. Do not reconstruct a fictitious contemporaneous notebook. Late recollections must be marked as retrospective notes.

Attach actual prompt/response logs and identify generated files. Preserve the original frozen archive and the new audit outputs separately. Record this audit as AI-assisted research review dated September 15, 2026.

## 13. Final submission self-audit

Every number has a denominator and evidence location. Every important sentence maps to A/B or explicitly labeled C/D in the claim ledger. All X claims are removed. Student has personally read cited work. AI contributions are disclosed. Eligibility and anonymity are checked. Protected outcomes remain sealed. The student can reproduce a small example without software and explain every plotted quantity. Official approvals are authentic. Only then is this ready for submission.
